Why I'm Researching AI and Study Skills

Every year, a new wave of students arrives at university already using AI tools — for summarising readings, planning essays, checking their own understanding. What's less clear is whether that use actually helps them learn, or just makes the process of avoiding learning more efficient.

That question sits behind my Honours research. I'm working on a systematic literature review, supervised by Dr Neil Evans, that looks at the relationship between Artificial Intelligence and study skills in higher education. Specifically, I'm interested in the conditions — not just the tools — that determine whether AI use supports genuine learning or quietly undermines it.

It's a question I come to from a few directions at once. As a peer tutor, I see how students actually work, not just how we assume they work. As someone running study skills workshops through Elevate Education, I spend a lot of time thinking about the habits and strategies that separate students who cope from students who thrive. And as an IS&T student myself, I'm naturally drawn to the systems view: what conditions, incentives, and design choices shape how a technology gets used.

What I don't want to do is treat AI as either a threat to be defended against or a shortcut to be celebrated. Both framings skip the more useful question: under what conditions does it actually help someone learn? That's the gap I'm trying to work in.

I'll be sharing more as the literature review takes shape — what the existing research says, where it disagrees, and where I think there's still an open question worth asking.
