Top 5 hot IT trends for 2026: a synthesis

A comparative reading of Gartner's Top Strategic Technology Trends for 2026 against Forrester, Microsoft, SAP Africa, and Deloitte — with a South African lens throughout.

Introduction

Every year, industry analyst firms publish lists of the technology trends they expect to shape organisational strategy over the next twelve to eighteen months. These lists are widely read and widely cited, but they are also produced by firms with their own methodologies, client bases, and commercial interests, so no single list should be treated as a neutral summary of "what is happening" in technology. This synthesis works from Gartner's Top Strategic Technology Trends for 2026, presented at Gartner's IT Symposium/Xpo (Gartner, 2025), as a starting point, and narrows a broader initial list of candidate trends down to five for deeper analysis. Each trend is then read against at least one other analyst or industry source to test where the frameworks agree, where they diverge, and what that divergence suggests about the maturity of the trend. Each section closes by considering how the trend is landing, or is likely to land, in South Africa specifically, since global technology narratives do not translate evenly into economies with different infrastructure, skills, and investment conditions.

1. Multiagent and agentic AI systems

Gartner (2025) frames multiagent systems as a practical route for organisations to automate complex business processes by coordinating several smaller, specialised AI agents rather than relying on one large, general-purpose model. Forrester's independent top-10 emerging technology list for 2025 arrives at a closely related trend from a different angle: agentic AI, which Forrester vice president Brian Hopkins describes as the underlying technologies — reasoning models, LLMs acting as critics, advanced retrieval methods, and novel model-engineering techniques — that make AI agents more autonomous (Shein, 2025). The overlap between two competing analyst firms independently placing a version of this idea at or near the top of their 2025/2026 lists is itself a signal worth noting: it suggests the trend is not a single firm's branding exercise, but a genuine shift analysts across the industry are observing in client demand.

Where the two framings differ is instructive. Gartner's language is organisational and process-oriented — multiagent systems as a way to automate "complex business processes." Forrester's language is more technical and infrastructural — agentic AI as a set of underlying capabilities that make agents more autonomous. Read together, this suggests two layers of the same trend: a technical layer (the models and techniques Forrester describes) that is what makes possible the organisational layer (the process automation Gartner describes).

In South Africa, this trend intersects directly with a well-documented digital divide. Microsoft's Global AI Diffusion Q1 2026 report found that 23.1% of South Africa's working-age population had used a generative AI product, placing the country 46th of 147 economies measured, but ahead of every other African economy in the dataset (cited in ITWeb, 2026; TechCentral, 2026). The same report attributes the gap between the Global North and Global South largely to unreliable electricity, limited internet connectivity, and gaps in digital skills (TechCentral, 2026) — constraints that apply with particular force to multiagent systems, which typically require more reliable compute and connectivity than a single chatbot interface.

2. Domain-specific language models

Gartner (2025) describes domain-specific language models as large language models fine-tuned on data from one field — law, medicine, education — rather than being general-purpose. The claimed advantage is accuracy and regulatory compliance in narrow, specialised use cases. Be Informed (2025), an independent industry commentator summarising the same Gartner list, goes further and singles this out as "the rising star" of the entire 2026 list — a useful piece of corroborating evidence that the idea is resonating beyond Gartner's own framing.

A reasonable critique is that "fine-tuned for a domain" is not new in machine learning generally; what is new is the commercial packaging of it as a distinct, investable category, separate from general-purpose foundation models. For South Africa, this framing is relevant to sectors under pressure to adopt AI responsibly and compliantly, particularly given South Africa's POPIA obligations — a domain-tuned model with narrower, more auditable outputs may be an easier internal compliance case than a general-purpose model.

3. AI-native development platforms

This trend describes generative AI built directly into the software development process — not a plugin bolted onto an existing IDE, but tooling designed around AI assistance from the outset (Gartner, 2025), letting smaller teams build software faster with less traditional hand-coding. This is one of the more empirically verifiable trends on the list: Microsoft's own Q1 2026 AI Diffusion report identifies software development as "the clearest sign of AI's near-term economic impact," citing rapid growth in AI-assisted code production and repository creation (IT-Online, 2026) — a usage-telemetry claim that converges with Gartner's analyst-driven framing from an entirely different methodology.

South Africa's own developer base is a relevant local data point: Boston Consulting Group estimated roughly 4.7 million developers across Africa as of 2024 (cited in IT-Online, 2026), a workforce AI-native tooling could meaningfully leverage — though the same connectivity constraints noted above apply here too, since cloud-based AI coding assistants depend on consistent connectivity not uniformly available outside major metros.

4. Physical AI

Gartner (2025) describes physical AI as artificial intelligence moving out of purely digital contexts into physical systems — robots, drones, smart equipment. Forrester's parallel 2025 list supports the direction of this trend but places its most visible expression, humanoid robots, in its long-term category, five or more years from delivering tangible enterprise value (Shein, 2025) — a useful corrective to Gartner's framing, which presents physical AI as a current strategic trend to plan for now.

Deloitte's Africa-focused commentary adds a regional dimension not covered by either Gartner or Forrester: its Africa technology trends discussion argues that "Africa must take spatial computing seriously," a category closely related to physical AI, as a matter of strategic urgency. Set against South Africa's continuing electricity-supply issues, physical AI is arguably the trend on this list where the gap between the global narrative and local operating conditions is widest.

5. Preemptive cybersecurity

Gartner (2025) frames preemptive cybersecurity as a shift away from reactive security — detecting and responding after a breach — toward using AI to predict and block threats before they happen, in direct response to a documented rise in attacks. IBM's data breach research put the global average cost of a breach at $4.4 million in 2025 (cited in SAP Africa News, 2026), giving organisations a concrete financial reason to prioritise this shift.

South African commentary on the same pressure frames it slightly differently: SAP's 2026 analysis of African SMEs argues that cloud platforms, through consolidated, professionally managed patching and monitoring, are becoming the practical route to stronger security for smaller organisations that cannot build in-house preemptive capability from scratch (SAP Africa News, 2026). For the roughly 95% of sub-Saharan African businesses that are SMEs (SAP Africa News, 2026), the more accessible near-term path to preemptive security may be indirect — buying into cloud platforms that have already built it in.

Conclusion

Read together, these five trends point to a common thread: the most credible 2026 trends are the ones where two or more independently produced sources converge on the same underlying shift, even when they frame it differently. Multiagent/agentic AI and AI-native development platforms both show this convergence and are correspondingly the trends with the clearest near-term evidence. Physical AI shows the widest gap between the confidence of its headline framing and the caution of more detailed timelines further down the same sources. Across all five, South Africa's position is consistent: ahead of the rest of the African continent on adoption, but still constrained by the same foundational gaps in electricity, connectivity, and digital skills that limit how much of any global trend can be realised locally in the near term.
