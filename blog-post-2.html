What Peer Tutoring Taught Me About Communication

Databases and Programming is not a module people take for fun. Most of the students I tutor for ISTN212 arrive at my sessions tired, a little behind, and quietly convinced they're not "technical people." My job isn't really to re-teach the lecture. It's to figure out where the explanation broke down the first time, and say it differently.

That's harder than it sounds. The instinct, when you understand something, is to explain it the way it finally clicked for you. But that path is personal — it depends on what you already knew, what you were confused about, and what example happened to land. Good tutoring means building a few different paths to the same idea, and reading the room well enough to know which one someone needs.

I've learned to slow down at the exact point where I want to speed up. The moment a concept feels obvious to explain is usually the moment I'm least aware of how much I'm assuming. Normalisation, joins, query optimisation — these aren't hard because the ideas are complicated. They're hard because each one quietly depends on getting the last one right, and it's easy to lose someone without noticing.

The other thing tutoring taught me: confidence and competence are not the same thing, and they don't always arrive together. Some of the students who understand the material best are the ones who trust themselves least. Part of the job is just being consistent enough, patient enough, that trust has room to catch up.

None of this is unique to databases. It's the same skill I lean on running Study Skills workshops, and the same one I'll need in any technical role — translating something true and complicated into something someone else can actually use.
